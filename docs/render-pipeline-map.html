THE PROBLEM, IN PLAIN TERMS

What the player sees

Base Game runs at roughly 45 frames a second when standing still and dips below 20 while walking. The dips are the complaint. The standing rate matters too, because it leaves little headroom: a frame budget of 22 ms at 45 fps means anything extra pushes a frame over 33 ms, which is a visible stutter.

Where the time goes

Almost none of it is the GPU. The graphics card finishes its work in about 1 ms. The frame is spent on the CPU, inside Three's render call, turning about 200 scene objects into GPU commands. In the captures that step alone takes 12 to 42 ms.

The reason is structural rather than a bug. Before drawing each object, Three asks whether the object needs its shader inputs refreshed. Its answer is yes for any material built from a node graph, because it cannot see inside the graph to know what might have changed. Every material in this game is that kind. So every frame, every object gets its uniforms re-checked, its bindings re-walked and its pipeline re-verified, whether or not anything about it moved. About 45% of the render step is that binding walk and about 17% the node updates, for objects that are mostly static terrain, buildings and trees.

The steady cost is a per-object tax the engine charges because it cannot tell static from dynamic. It scales with how many objects we draw, not with how much changes.

Why it dips while walking

Walking streams terrain. Terrain tiles are built on worker threads, and the page used to start sixteen of them at once when a chunk boundary was crossed. On a machine with ten-plus cores that still slowed the main thread roughly in half for the duration, and a DevTools trace showed nearly every long frame overlapping such a burst. Capping the shared pool at two threads removed that penalty within a capture. Spikes remain at two threads and still line up with terrain work and long tasks, so worker activity is not fully explained, and one session's spikes were gaps where the browser simply did not schedule the next frame. A separate, rarer hitch is a shader compiling the first time a material is drawn: one frame took 1.5 s that way.

What the proposed changes should do

  • Draw fewer objects. Every object pays the per-object refresh, so merging the forest's far-branch meshes into one draw per role cuts the largest group from 144 meshes toward a handful. Turning the forest off entirely saved about 8 ms, which bounds the prize; what one merged role saves is unknown until the same-content comparison runs. The prototype exists behind a default-off option; it must first be shown to render identically and to cost less on the GPU than the draws it saves.
  • Answer the refresh question ourselves for objects we can prove static. Tried as a non-skipping audit first. The audit ran 33,000 frames and found that while the camera moves, every static object's refresh writes a necessary uniform (its model-view matrix depends on the camera), and the check itself cost more than the work it could skip. Marked not worth building in this form. A separate, bounded idea remains open: keep camera and pass data apart from stable object transforms and combine them in the shader, so the per-object upload is not redone for camera motion; its shader, memory and Three-integration costs are unassessed.
  • Stop paying twice for identical state. Share materials that are the same, and upload per-frame uniforms once per pass instead of once per mesh. Small, correctness-neutral, already in.
  • Keep the workers from starving the main thread, and count what terrain uploads and keeps resident, so the next decisions there rest on numbers rather than comments.
  • Measure first. The trace now splits the render step into its six stages, counts refreshes and writes, names any shader compile, and records what the device granted. Nothing above is accepted until a browser capture agrees with the Node results.

What would count as success

Not a higher frame rate on its own. A change is accepted when it is visually identical, its correctness is established for the inputs it touches, and its cost and benefit are both measured. A change that lowers memory or removes a wrong cost model without moving the frame rate still counts. The current 45 fps is the working baseline, not the acceptance bar.

DEFINITIONS

Two words this map turns on. The encode is the CPU time Three spends turning the scene into GPU commands each frame; it is most of the frame here. A RenderObject is Three's record for one (object, material, pass) combination, and every one of them pays the per-object stages in Part 2.
